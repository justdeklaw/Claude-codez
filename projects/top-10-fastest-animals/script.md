# Top 10 Fastest Animals on Earth

**Working title:** The 10 FASTEST Animals on Earth (#1 Breaks the Speed of a Race Car)
**Length target:** ~8 min long-form · each rank also becomes a ~40 s Short
**Recording tips:** record each block as its own file → `voiceover/00-hook.wav`, `voiceover/10.wav` … `voiceover/01.wav`, `voiceover/99-outro.wav`. Leave 1 s of silence at the start and end of each file. Speak with energy; a slight pause after each number.

> Speeds are approximate and rounded; animal top speeds are hard to measure, and the script says so where scientists disagree.

---

## 00 — Hook (≈15 s)
One of the animals on this list can hit nearly **390 kilometres per hour** — faster than a Formula 1 car on a straight. Another is a mammal most people have never seen during the day, and it out-flies almost every bird alive. Stick around, because number one is not the animal you think it is.

## 10 — Greyhound (~72 km/h · 45 mph)
Number ten: the greyhound. Bred for thousands of years to chase prey, the greyhound is the fastest dog on the planet, reaching around seventy-two kilometres per hour. Its secret is the "double suspension gallop" — twice in every stride, all four feet leave the ground. A greyhound can hit top speed in just six strides. And yet, off the track, most greyhounds are famous couch potatoes that sleep up to eighteen hours a day.

## 09 — Lion (~80 km/h · 50 mph)
Number nine: the lion. The king of the savanna can explode to about eighty kilometres per hour — but only for a few seconds. Lions have massive muscles but relatively small hearts for their size, which means they tire fast. That's why lions hunt in teams and stalk to within about thirty metres before they sprint. If the first rush fails, the hunt is usually over.

## 08 — Pronghorn (~88 km/h · 55 mph)
Number eight: the pronghorn. It isn't the fastest sprinter, but it may be the best runner on Earth. Found in North America, the pronghorn can reach around eighty-eight kilometres per hour — and hold over sixty-five kilometres per hour for several kilometres. That's something no cheetah could do. Scientists think it evolved this speed to escape the American cheetah, a predator that went extinct more than ten thousand years ago. The pronghorn is still running from a ghost.

## 07 — Cheetah (~105 km/h · 65 mph)
Number seven: the cheetah — the fastest land animal alive. In a burst, a cheetah can top roughly one hundred kilometres per hour, and it can go from zero to ninety-five faster than many sports cars. Its spine flexes like a spring, its claws grip like running spikes, and its long tail acts as a rudder for sharp turns. But that speed has a price: after a chase, a cheetah must rest so long that lions and hyenas often steal its meal.

## 06 — Sailfish (~110 km/h claimed · 68 mph)
Number six: the sailfish, often called the fastest fish in the ocean. You'll often see the figure of one hundred and ten kilometres per hour — but here's the honest truth: newer studies suggest real hunting speeds are much lower, and scientists are still debating it. What's not in doubt is how it hunts. Sailfish raise their giant sail-like fin to herd schools of sardines, then slash through them with their spear-like bill. They can even change colour while they hunt.

## 05 — Common Swift (~111 km/h · 69 mph, level flight)
Number five: the common swift. In 2010, researchers in Sweden measured swifts flying at over one hundred and eleven kilometres per hour — in level flight, no diving. But speed is not even the craziest thing about them. A swift can stay in the air for up to ten months without landing. It eats, drinks, and even sleeps while flying. Some young swifts don't touch the ground for years.

## 04 — Brazilian Free-Tailed Bat (~160 km/h · 99 mph)
Number four: the Brazilian free-tailed bat. In 2016, scientists tracked these bats with tiny radio tags and recorded bursts of about one hundred and sixty kilometres per hour in level flight — a speed that challenged the idea that birds rule the sky. These bats live in enormous colonies; one cave in Texas holds around fifteen million of them. When they leave at sunset, the swarm is so large it shows up on weather radar.

## 03 — White-Throated Needletail (~170 km/h claimed · 105 mph)
Number three: the white-throated needletail, a kind of swift. Old reports claim it can reach around one hundred and seventy kilometres per hour in level flight, which would make it the fastest flapping bird. Those measurements have never been fully confirmed, so take it with a pinch of salt. Still, anyone who has seen one tear across the sky agrees: it looks less like a bird and more like a missile with feathers.

## 02 — Golden Eagle (~240+ km/h in a dive · 150 mph)
Number two: the golden eagle. In a hunting dive, called a stoop, a golden eagle can exceed two hundred and forty kilometres per hour. Its eyesight is roughly four to eight times sharper than ours, letting it spot a rabbit from more than a kilometre away. Golden eagles have been known to take down prey as large as young deer — that's speed and power combined.

## 01 — Peregrine Falcon (~390 km/h in a dive · 240 mph)
Number one: the peregrine falcon — the fastest animal on the planet. When it dives, it folds its wings into a teardrop shape and can exceed three hundred and twenty kilometres per hour. One trained falcon was recorded at around three hundred and eighty-nine kilometres per hour. At those speeds, special bony cones in its nostrils help control the air rushing in, and a third eyelid protects its eyes. It strikes its prey in mid-air with a blow that is often instantly fatal.
